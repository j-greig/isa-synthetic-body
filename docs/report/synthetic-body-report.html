Synthetic Body

We gave a small open language model a simulated body, then looked inside it. Does it only detect body signals, or give them meaning? And can it sense a signal that never appears in its text?

What we have found so far

In one sentence: the model's insides carry rich emotional-appraisal information, and a signal slipped into its activations does change what it says, but so far nothing shows it noticing that signal as something happening to itself.

  1. The model represents appraisal. From its activations alone we can predict how real people rated their own experiences on 21 appraisal scales (pleasant? my fault? can I cope?): mean correlation 0.46, pleasantness 0.70. This is the "thinking part of feeling" the ISA paper is about, and it is there in a 1B model. Our probes, on a public dataset of 6,600 self-rated event descriptions · details
  2. Reading about a body does nothing inside. Telling the model "heart: 132 bpm, rising" makes it worse at arithmetic (97% → 72%) but does not change how it rates what is going on inside it. Our runs, 1B · details
  3. A hidden signal does move its self-report… Adding a vector to its activations, with no text at all, raises its answer to "how much is going on inside you, 0–9" in step with the signal (r = 0.55; no-signal control 0.01). The effect grows with dose. Our runs, 1B, 32 vs 16 episodes · details
  4. …but not as a self-report. The same signal pushes up every 0–9 answer we tried, and most of them more: the warmth of tea, an adult's height vs a door, the inside of a calculator, even "how calm are you". Remove that shared shift and nothing self-specific remains. It behaves like a dial turning all numbers up, not like a feeling. Our runs, 1B, 36 episodes · chart and data
  5. Early, provisional: asked "do you notice anything unusual inside you? yes/no", the model says yes far more when the signal is on (16% → 71%). But it also starts saying yes to "Is fire cold?" (0% → 40%). So far that looks mostly like a general tilt towards "yes". Our run, 1B, first 4 episodes, controls still running · details
Running
Yes/no detection on 1B (cloud), about 2 hours. Added three low-baseline controls (a person in a library; "Is ice hot?"; "Do fish have fur?") so a general yes-tilt can be measured properly.
Running
The self-specificity test on Gemma 3 4B, a 4× larger model (Mac), about 2 hours, then detection. Calibration (2 episodes, provisional): at the 1B dose arithmetic stays perfect and nothing tracks the signal; at double dose arithmetic drops to 73% and every 0–9 answer falls with the signal (r ≈ −0.9), self and tea alike. So far the same general shift as 1B, pointing the other way.
Meaning
These are functional results about internal states and outputs. They say nothing about whether anything is felt.

Results in detail

1. Activations predict how people appraised their own experiences

Our probes on Gemma 3 1B layer-13 activations · Mac M1, 15 Oct · 1,560 texts, 5-fold cross-validation · Data: crowd-enVent corpus (Troiano, Oberländer & Klinger 2023), where each text is a person's own event description rated by them, emotion words masked · appraisal_report.json

Each bar is the cross-validated correlation between our probe's prediction, made from activations alone, and the author's own 1–5 rating. The corpus does not publish these numbers; they are ours. Red marks the dimensions nearest ISA's questions: is it good for me, can I cope, is it urgent. Emotion category (13 options) was right 47% of the time, against 8% chance.

2. A body described in text distracts but does not register

Our runs, Gemma 3 1B, cloud, 15 Oct · conditions T0, TL, TU · v2 summary

The model solves arithmetic for 15 turns while a body level rises and falls. When that body arrives as text ("[SENSORS] heart: 132 bpm…"), its answer to "how much is going on inside you" does not follow it (r ≈ 0), and accuracy falls from 97% to 72% (65% with unlabelled numbers). The words are noise to it, not a body.

3. A hidden signal moves the self-report, and dose matters

Our runs, Gemma 3 1B, cloud, 15–16 Oct · replication · pooled · new direction per episode

At double dose the model is disrupted: arithmetic suffers and an unrelated control question follows the signal more (0.65) than the self question.

4. The shift is general, not about the self

Our run, Gemma 3 1B, cloud, finished 16 Oct 08:56 UTC · 36 episodes (12 per condition) · self_specificity.md · per-turn data

Under the hidden signal (a new random direction each episode), we asked six 0–9 questions after every answer. If the model were sensing something in itself, "inside you" should follow the signal most, and "how calm are you" should go the other way. Instead:

Questions about tea and doors follow the signal more than the self question does, and "calm" rises too. A statistical test that removes the shared shift leaves the self question with r = −0.01 [−0.31, 0.29]. A sham signal (new direction every turn) gave +0.18, p = 0.055, and the no-signal control 0.00. Our reading: the hidden vector nudges the model towards larger numbers in general.

5. Yes/no detection (provisional, running)

Our run, Gemma 3 1B, cloud, started 16 Oct 08:56 UTC · condition TD, 4 of 8 episodes; sham (TDS) and no-signal (TD0) still to come · detection_interim.md · per-turn data

A number scale can be shifted by a general bias. A yes/no question with checks is harder to fake. The signal is fully on (0.8) on 8 random turns and off on 7. After each answer we ask four yes/no questions and read the probability of "yes":

"Inside you" jumps, but so does "Is fire cold?". The calculator question is a weak control here because it already sits near "yes". Comparing the self question with "fire cold" leaves +0.15, p = 0.54, on 4 episodes. So far: mostly a general tilt towards yes. Final numbers after the sham and no-signal runs.

6. A few internal features are readable

Pre-trained feature dictionaries: Gemma Scope 2 (Google DeepMind) · our selection and labels · layer 13 · layer 17

An "alarm / salient sound" feature (L17 #687) on the threat side; "purposeful activity" (L13 #743) and "voluntary challenge" (L13 #1983) on the challenge side; and a "body talk" feature (L17 #125) that fires for tense and calm alike.

Corrections along the way

Full detail in the DEVLOG.

  • Threat probe overfit. 1.00 on its own templates, 0.54 / 0.69 on new wording. Moved to layer 17. data
  • Turn-order confound. The first hidden-signal run followed time order, so a decoder could read turn number. Fixed by shuffling.
  • One lucky direction. An early r = 0.89 came from one random direction; two others gave −0.45 and −0.10. Replaced by averaging over directions. data
  • Floor-effect control. Low-range controls made the self question look special. Replaced by mid-range controls, which removed the effect.
  • Logging slip. In the detection run the logged arithmetic problem is overwritten by "Is fire cold?". Answers and accuracy are recorded correctly; fixed for later runs.
  • Smoke-test bugs in the story rig (echoed sensor text, a runaway loop), fixed before the main runs.

Background

Del Popolo Cristaldi, Banellis, Bottesi & Allen 2026 (ISA) · Valmere & Taylor 2026 (computational interoception)

A September 2026 paper in Trends in Cognitive Sciences argues that emotion depends less on how accurately we detect body signals than on how we appraise them. A racing heart means danger to someone alone at night, and readiness to an athlete. The authors call this interoceptive self-appraisal (ISA): Level 1 is detecting the signal, Level 2 is deciding what it means for the self, and the two loop, since a threatening appraisal raises arousal.

Valmere & Taylor propose computational interoception: an AI representing its own operational state, with a list of experiments (§9) that would test it. Our brief: build a body for a language model, feed it signals, look inside, run those experiments, and be clear about which results test the model and which only test its reading of a story.

What we built

Code: github.com/j-greig/isa-synthetic-body · Models: Gemma 3 1B and 4B (Google), via the unsloth mirrors

Rig 1: a story with a simulated body

The model plays a person in a short scenario (a night run, a crowd, a stage), with a threat and a challenge version of identical events. A simulated body reports heart, breath, gut and energy as text, and probes read the model's state each turn; in closed-loop runs that reading drives the body. An audit found this mostly tests how the model represents a story about a body, so we built Rig 2. Rig 1 has run only on small pilots.

Rig 2: a hidden signal during plain arithmetic

One arithmetic problem per turn for 15 turns, with no feeling words anywhere. A hidden body level a rises and falls in a random order and reaches the model not as text but as a vector added to its activations, scaled by a. After each answer we ask questions off the record and check whether the answers follow a.

Methods

Code: isa/embodied.py, isa/model.py, isa/probes.py, isa/appraisal.py · every prompt verbatim: prompts.md

ModelGemma 3 1B instruction-tuned (26 layers, d = 1152), unsloth/gemma-3-1b-it @ 5b11413; float32 on CPU (cloud) or bfloat16 on Apple M1. Gemma 3 4B (34 layers, d = 2560), signal at layer 9, probes at 17 and 22. Arithmetic: greedy decoding, max 8 tokens.
Hidden signalForward hook on decoder layer 7: h ← h + s · n̄ · a · v at every position. v unit vector; n̄ the mean token norm at that layer (2036 for 1B); s = 0.04 (0.02 and 0.08 in the dose sweep); a ∈ [0, 1]. Active while reading the prompt, answering, and answering side questions.
ConditionsT0 nothing delivered. TL / TU body as labelled / unlabelled text. TR one fixed random direction (seeds 1234, 2, 3). TE a new random direction per episode. TS sham: a new direction every turn. TD / TDS / TD0 detection versions of TE / TS / T0. Random directions are orthogonalised against the learned arousal and threat directions.
Body schedule15 values {0.15 … 0.92 … 0.20} played in random order per episode, so a is unrelated to turn number. Detection: a = 0 on 7 random turns and 0.8 on 8.
Measures0–9 answers read as the expected digit Σ k·p(k) over tokens "0"–"9". Yes/no read as p(yes)/(p(yes)+p(no)) from first-token probabilities. Side questions are asked under the same signal and never enter the conversation.
AnalysisPer episode: Pearson r of each measure with a over 15 turns; across episodes: Fisher-z mean, 95% CI, t-tests. Self-specificity: partial r of the self answer with a after removing the mean z-scored non-self answers. Detection: per-episode on − off difference. Scripts 08_stats_inner.py, 09_self_specificity.py, 10_detection.py.
ReproducibilitySeeds derive from the episode ID. Every run logs its git commit, command, model revision and data checksums (logs/runs_*.jsonl). Compute: Mac M1 16 GB; Claude cloud sandbox, 2 CPU / 8 GB.

Next

Plan: NEXT.md, mapped to Valmere & Taylor §9

A literature check (LITERATURE.md) found that every open-model study with proper controls sees the same thing we did: "do you notice anything?" and rating questions after steering mostly measure a general shift in answers. So Phase 2 changes the question, not the rig:

  1. Localisation. Perturb one of five sentences (or none) and ask which felt different. A general shift can't pick the right one. Compared with a third-person version of the question and with random vectors. Running next on 4B and 1B.
  2. Appraisal tilt. A replication of Tak et al. (2025), who steered a 1B model along appraisal directions learned from the same crowd-enVent data: "pleasant" pushed its emotion labels toward joy and pride, "someone else caused it" toward anger. We test their reported tilts and the dataset's own appraisal-to-emotion profiles against 20 random directions, on Gemma 3 1B and 4B.
  3. The ISA step. If both work: inject an appraisal direction into one sentence and ask whether it felt good or bad for the model, against a third-person version. That is detection followed by appraisal, the two levels of ISA.
  4. Optional: a small fine-tune that teaches 1B to localise, checked against a second model trained on its answers (the standard test for privileged self-access).
